A transition area is exactly what it sounds like: the place where one kind of country turns into another. It is a concept the pike anglers of Europe and the largemouth bass scene in the States talk about all the time, and it applies just as well to barra.

In the Top End, examples look like this:

- A mud bank turning into a collapsed bank
- A gravel bar turning into a mud bank
- A shallow weed bed morphing into a lily line in a billabong
- A run of mangroves meeting a beach
- A smooth mud flat running into a rock bar

Anything to do with rock walls, rock bars and gravel banks will have a transition at either end. Those ends are great places to start looking.

## Why they work

It is very hard for a predator to catch bait along a long, uniform stretch of bank. Bait schools moving up and down the waterline with the tide settle into a pattern and stay in it. A transition breaks that pattern. The change from flat mud to rock gives the current something to swirl around, often throws out a colour change on a run-out or run-in tide, and changes the way the bait swims for a beat or two. That moment is all a barra needs.

There is a simpler side to it as well. Fish, like plenty of other animals, gravitate to anything that stands out in an otherwise featureless environment. In a river that is all rock, the one log will hold fish. In an estuary that is all mud, the one rock bar will. A single stick in the middle of a flat, or a couple of lonely stumps out in deeper water in an impoundment, can hold a surprising number of fish. They do not have to be big features. They just have to be different.

## In the billabongs

A weed bed that turns into a drop-off and a lily-pad line is a classic barra hot spot. Bait come and go between the warmer shallow water and the deep, and barra have access to both depending on their needs and mood. Look for bays with deeper water right beside them so the fish have both options — up the back of the bay when it is cool first thing, and down the front as it warms.

## In the harbour and the estuaries

Transitions are one of our big go-tos for guided fishing in the estuaries. If there is not an active barra willing to cooperate at that moment, there is a good chance of other predators — golden snapper, trevally, queenfish, blue salmon, threadfin and more. And at some stage of the tide, particularly through a spring tide cycle, barra will use those same transition areas.

Right through Darwin Harbour, almost anywhere there is a point there is a rock bar, and anywhere there are rocks there is a transition from mud to rock. A drain pulling a small colour change off a rocky stretch, a gravel patch at the top of a creek, a mangrove clump standing out from a big mud flat — these are the spots that come alive at particular stages of the tide. Some fish well on a falling tide, some only on the very first of the push back in, and some for a short, sharp window before you even notice the water moving. Harbour barra are fickle, but there are a lot more of them than most people think, and the anglers who put the hours in to learn the timing are the ones who catch them.

Bridges, wharves and beaches all have their own transitions, and once you start looking for them they will stand out to you as high-percentage barra country at various stages of the tide.

## Reading the bank from a distance

A tip worth keeping: on a river you have never fished, you can often pick a rock bar by a change in the vegetation on the banks. Different trees grow where the ground changes, and a strip of taller, darker trees coming in from both sides of the river often has a rock bar running between them. It is the same idea as reading Google Earth for deep holes — the land gives the water away.

## Pole position

The fish sitting on the pressure wave in front of a piece of structure — at the up-current end, where the water first hits it — tend to be the more active ones, looking for a feed and more likely to hit a lure. The ones tucked in behind are more likely resting. On a big snag that means the root end; on a transition it means the edge where the current first meets the change.

## Write it down

Plenty of good anglers fish transitions without ever naming them — they notice a pattern almost subconsciously and go back to it. You get better when you notice it on purpose. Observation is the first step, recognising the pattern is the second, and being able to repeat it is the third.

These days that is easy. Drop a mark on your sounder or phone app with a short note on the tide height and conditions, and your photos are date-stamped for you. What works becomes repeatable, and it becomes transferable to systems you have not fished yet.

@faq

Q: What is a transition area in barra fishing?
A: Anywhere one type of country changes into another — a mud bank turning into a collapsed bank, a gravel bar into mud, a weed bed into a lily line, mangroves into a beach, or a mud flat into a rock bar. Those changes break up the pattern of bait moving along the bank, which gives barramundi an opening to attack.

Q: Why do barramundi gather at transition areas?
A: Bait moving along a uniform bank is hard to catch. Where the structure changes, the current swirls, colour changes form and the bait gets thrown off its pattern for a moment. Fish also simply gravitate to anything that stands out in an otherwise featureless environment.

Q: Do transition areas work in freshwater billabongs?
A: Yes. A shallow weed bed that drops off into a lily-pad line is a classic barra spot, because bait move between the warmer shallows and the deeper water, and barra can use both depending on their mood. Look for bays with deeper water right beside them.

Q: How can you find a rock bar on an unfamiliar river?
A: Look at the vegetation on the banks. A strip of taller, darker trees coming down to the water on both sides often marks a rock bar running across the river between them.
