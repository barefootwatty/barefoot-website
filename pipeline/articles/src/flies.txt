Some anglers like big flies, some like small ones. Some like generic little baitfish patterns, and some like a crazy concoction of shrimp, fish and something closer to a fried chicken drumstick. All of them will trick a barra if you put it in front of his nose when he is hungry.

That is the same as any lure, apart from the ones that spook him. So the best bet is not to chase the one perfect pattern. **The best bet is a good variety.**

## Big and bold, or small and simple

Largely, we are big fans of bigger flies with a bit of attitude: something that gets the fish's attention and says, "I bet you wouldn't eat me."

But there are times when a small, simple Clouser will dominate a bite period on a flat, and it comes down mostly to the fish's mood on the day. Carry both, start with the one that suits the water, and change if you are being ignored.

## When and where to fish them

In general:

- **In the salt,** the run-out tide is best. We are usually looking for a falling tide in the morning, through the middle of the spring tides
- **In the fresh,** first thing in the morning and last thing in the afternoon
- **In impoundments,** the shallow weed beds can fish better as the sun gets up and warms the water

In the salt we also want somewhere the prevailing wind is not making life hard for casting, boat positioning and seeing the fish. We need clean water to spot them and cover for them to hunt in. The bigger single mangrove trees with large overhanging branches are best, along with collapsed banks and rocky edges. Get all three on one flat and you are in for a show.

Sight-casting a barra is perhaps the trickiest way to catch one. You need to land the fly ahead of and beyond the fish without spooking it, then bring it back across his nose to intercept him.

## Flies in the billabongs

Chasing barra on fly in the Top End billabongs follows the same principles as any lure casting. Look for the transition areas between weed, trees and lily pads. Fish right up the back of the pads and under the branches.

- **Subsurface flies:** let the fly sink as it drops off the face of the weed bed or the bank
- **Surface flies:** give the fish plenty of time to find it. Strip to pop, then wait three or four seconds before going again

That feels slow, but a hungry barra will happily swim five or ten metres from under the lily pads, with a couple of flicks of the tail, to eat a long-paused popper. You cover the water just as quickly, and twice as effectively, as you would casting and stripping like a madman.

## When a fly beats a lure

For us, a fly comes out pretty much every time we are fishing in under two metres of water. But there are two situations where it is clearly the better option:

- **On the flats, in shallow, clean water.** A fly lands softly and can be made to look exactly like what the fish are eating
- **During runoff, when barra are keyed on tiny food.** In the upper reaches of the rivers, baby cherabin (tiny freshwater prawns) flood down the edges in huge numbers, and barra will eat dozens at a time. Fish feeding like that, or on rainbowfish, are very hard to catch on anything else. A fly that matches them is probably the best option you have

That idea of matching the hatch applies well beyond barra. Out on the pelagics at the mouth of Darwin Harbour, metal slugs catch a few fish and soft plastics catch more. But a small, lightly dressed fly matched to exactly what the fish are eating can be the difference between a few bites and a fish every cast. You can still deliver it a long way from the boat, and match the bait in a way few lures can.

So, generally: when the fishing is tough, when the water is clean, or when you just like a challenge.

## Big flies that still cast

We do target runoff barra on fly, throwing big flies in exactly the same places we would throw plastics and hard bodies, usually on a shorter, stouter fly rod. The trick to casting big flies is tying them light.

- **Hollow tying.** Tie bucktail on facing forward, then force it back over itself so it flares out. It stands up and takes up plenty of room in the water but is hollow inside, so it weighs very little
- **Articulated flies.** Joining two or three segments gives a big fly lots of movement without much weight
- **High and tight profiles.** A craft fur fly can look big from the side while being almost nothing from the front. A smear of UV-cure resin worked through the fibres holds that shape even when wet, like hair gel, while the fly still moves freely

## Throwing flies on a spin rod

There is a lot of crossover these days. Many of the bigger flies can be thrown on a light spin rod with 10 or 15 pound braid. Jointed baitfish flies and articulated shrimp patterns work well this way, and a small ball sinker in the loop knot gets them down.

An articulated shrimp fished like that is a good option for aggregated fish in Shoal Bay. It hits the bottom, waves around enticingly, then jigs up and does its shrimpy thing as you lift it. Smaller shrimp patterns with tungsten dumbbell eyes cast fine on spin gear too.

On the surface, a walk-the-dog style foam popper with a simple weed guard, made by pushing mono through the foam, is very castable on spin gear. In the billabongs it is not only barra that will come a long way for one. Saratoga will too.

Marabou jigs are the same idea in a different package. They are feathers tied on a jig head, and for years they were a dynamite barra lure when the fishing was tough. The old saying holds up: if you cannot catch a barra, tie on something with a feather on it. We tend to tie our flies on plain hooks instead, and add a ball sinker in the loop knot on the rare occasion we need to get deeper than the fly line will take them.

## Getting into tying

Fly tying is a real addiction, so be warned. You do not need much to start. A cheap kit will get you going, and a vice with a rotary function, where the jaws spin, is available for well under a hundred dollars. More expensive vices hold hooks harder, but they are not essential. There are also local clubs and social groups around Darwin that hold fly-tying days, which is a good place to learn the basics.

It is a good thing to do in the wet season, and once you start you will want to tie flies all day, every day.

@faq

Q: What is the best fly for barramundi?
A: There is no single best fly. Big flies with a bit of attitude are great for getting a barra's attention, but there are days when a small, simple Clouser dominates on a flat. Carry a good variety and match it to the water and the fish's mood.

Q: When should I use a fly instead of a lure for barra?
A: In shallow, clean water under about two metres, especially on the flats, and during runoff when barra are feeding on tiny food such as baby cherabin or rainbowfish. Those fish are very hard to catch on anything that does not match what they are eating.

Q: How do you fish a popper fly for barra in a billabong?
A: Cast right up the back of the lily pads and under the branches, strip to pop, then wait three or four seconds before going again. A hungry barra will swim five or ten metres from under the pads to eat a long-paused popper.

Q: Can you cast barra flies on a spin rod?
A: Yes. Many bigger flies, including jointed baitfish patterns and articulated shrimp, can be thrown on a light spin rod with 10 or 15 pound braid. A small ball sinker in the loop knot helps get them down.
