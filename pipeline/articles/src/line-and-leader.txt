Walk into a tackle shop and there are rows and rows of braid and leader to choose from. Most of that choice does not matter. What matters is a simple rule, and a few adjustments through the year.

The rule is this: **use the lightest line you can confidently work with in any given situation**. Lighter line casts further, gets more bites in clear water and lets you fish smaller lures properly. But there are times when it pays to have real stopping power in your system, and knowing when those are is most of the skill.

## Two outfits, two jobs

We fish two outfits for barra, a spin and a baitcaster, and they carry different line for a reason.

- **The spin** gets the lighter braid, because we are throwing lighter lures and smaller plastics on it
- **The baitcaster** gets the heavier braid, because we are throwing bigger lures, casting more aggressively and generally targeting bigger fish with it

That split holds all year. What changes is how heavy each one goes.

## Choosing your braid

Brand does not matter much. There are plenty of good braids out there. What does matter, more than most people would think, is **how many strands make up the line**.

We run a five-carrier braid on everything these days. Compared with an eight or twelve-carrier braid, a five-carrier is far more durable and abrasion resistant. It still handles well, casts well and ties good knots, but it has a more workmanlike personality, and that is the line we trust.

There is a second reason that catches people out. An eight-carrier braid carries a lot more water back to the reel than a five-carrier does. Over time that water gets into the line roller bearing and the rest of the reel's components, and you start flogging them out. If you prefer an eight-carrier, there are some very good ones that cast like a dream and tie great knots. Just know you are giving up some abrasion resistance, and keep an eye on your reel.

## Braid through the year

This is how we set up the main line across the seasons:

- **Runoff baitcaster:** 50 pound braid
- **Runoff spin:** 20 pound braid
- **Dry season baitcaster:** 30 pound braid
- **Dry season spin:** 15 pound braid, dropping to 10 or even 8 when we need it

From about mid-February, when we start targeting runoff barra, the baitcasters get re-spooled with heavier line. That is purely because we are chasing bigger, more aggressive fish in dirtier water. In the coastal creeks and big runoff drains, the target fish is often 80 centimetres plus, and you need the horsepower to put the brakes on a big rampaging barra. Those fish are generally not shy of the line, because the water is dirty or at least tannin-stained.

The spin rods do not change much. We stick with 20 through the runoff, usually throwing smaller plastics at fish feeding on rainbowfish, small tadpoles or crabs.

The only time we go really light is in the middle of the dry, when the water is clean and cold and the barra get finicky and fussy. That is when the spare spools of 10 come out. In practice that tends to be on longer trips, when the fish have seen a lot and are spooky as anything. On a half day or a full day, 15 or 20 will generally get the job done.

## Choosing your leader

Leader is where you match the set-up to the situation, and it is worth choosing a leader for each one rather than leaving the same thing tied on all year. The trade-off is simple. Go heavier and you are less likely to get chewed off. Go lighter and, in clean water, you will get more bites.

- **Runoff, coastal creeks:** 70 pound and up. Some anglers go to 80, or even 100 in the big coastal creeks where huge barra are feeding aggressively. We would not go past 100, because castability suffers
- **The everyday go-to:** 55 to 60 pound. This is the run-of-the-mill barra leader through most of the year
- **Tougher conditions** in the billabongs or during runoff: you can drop to 45
- **Dry season, clean water:** 40 pound is the all-round go-to
- **Shallow, clear water sight-casting:** down to 30
- **Build-up:** up to 60 pound fluorocarbon

The build-up deserves a mention of its own. Those fish are getting really aggressive again, feeding hard in preparation for spawning in the wet, and you are very likely to get chewed off. The water is still clean, though, so we go to a heavier fluorocarbon rather than a heavy mono.

At the other end of the spectrum, in Darwin Harbour and Bynoe Harbour in June, when it is cold and the fish are not as aggressive, you can go as light as 30 pound. We would not go lighter than 20 as a leader. If you are worried about getting chewed off on a light leader, add a short **bite tippet** of 30, 40 or even 60 at the business end.

## Mono or fluorocarbon

As a rough rule we use mono in the heavier strains and fluorocarbon from 40 pound down. Fluorocarbon is designed to become close to invisible in the water, so it earns its place in clean, shallow, pressured water. Mono is more of a workhorse, and it is what we want for runoff and heavier fishing.

The exception is big fish in clean, shallow water, such as the build-up, or clear, deeper rocky points in the dry. That is where the 60 pound fluorocarbon comes out.

Not all fluorocarbon is equal. Some of it is brittle and will snap at the knot almost like clockwork at the end of a long day of casting, with the leader running in and out of the rod tip. If yours keeps doing that, try another brand before you blame your knots.

## What about trolling?

When you are trolling, don't worry too much about the fish being leader shy. You have an outboard about 50 metres away for a start, so the fish already know something is there. A barra that eats a trolled lure is hungry and wants it. We do most of our trolling in runoff, so we just troll with whatever mono leader is already tied on, usually the 55 or the 70. Fluorocarbon matters more when you are casting and finessing fish into a bite.

## Looking after it

A couple of small habits save a lot of grief:

- **Spool it on the way it came off.** When re-spooling, take the line off the supply spool in the same direction it was wound on. Lifting it off the end of a spool sideways is what puts twist in it
- **Get rid of twist as you move spots.** Cut off the lure, feed out double your usual cast length and drag the line behind the boat for a bit. It works for mono and fly lines too
- **Keep your leader spools tidy.** A line storage box makes a good leader box. If the line unravels inside, put a rubber band around the spool, give it a half twist and loop it back over so the line sits in the V. It holds the line in place every time you pull some off

## If you only buy two spools

Keep it simple. Rig your baitcasters with 30 pound braid and your spins with 20. Grab a spool of 55 pound mono leader for runoff and harder fishing, and a spool of 40 pound fluorocarbon for clean, shallow, dry-season water.

You can go down the rabbit hole from there, but you do not need to.

@faq

Q: What braid should I use for barramundi?
A: Use the lightest braid you can confidently work with. We run 50 pound on the baitcaster and 20 on the spin through runoff, then 30 on the baitcaster and 15 on the spin in the dry, dropping to 10 when the water is clean and the fish are fussy. We prefer a five-carrier braid for its abrasion resistance.

Q: What leader do you use for barra?
A: The everyday go-to is 55 to 60 pound. In runoff and the big coastal creeks we go to 70 pound and up, to a maximum of about 100. In clean dry-season water, 40 pound fluorocarbon is the all-rounder, dropping to 30 for shallow sight-casting.

Q: Should I use mono or fluorocarbon leader for barra?
A: Mono for the heavier strains, runoff and trolling, where the water is dirty and the fish are not line shy. Fluorocarbon from 40 pound down for clean, shallow, pressured water, plus 60 pound fluorocarbon for big aggressive fish in clear water, such as during the build-up.

Q: Is a five-carrier or eight-carrier braid better for barra?
A: Both work, but a five-carrier braid is generally more abrasion resistant and carries less water back to your reel, which is easier on the bearings. Eight-carriers cast beautifully and tie great knots, but give up some toughness.
