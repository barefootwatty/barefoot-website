Lures: how to choose the right one, in the right order

There is not a single lure that catches barra all the time. The best lure on any given day will be rubbish the next day, or in a different spot. Lure selection only gets complicated because we complicate it.

Walk into any tackle shop and the wall of lures is enough to bamboozle anyone. Most of that choice is unnecessary. What helps is a simple order of priorities, so that when a lure is not working you know what to change first.

The order that matters

This is how we prioritise lure selection, from most important to least:

  • Presentation: are you casting or trolling? Some lures cannot be cast well and some cannot be trolled, so this decides everything else
  • Size: most of the time, match the bait the barra are eating
  • Depth: put the lure at the level of the fish or just above it
  • Presence: how the lure feels in the water, which comes from its material and shape
  • Action: tight and fast, slow rolling, paddle tail, wedge tail
  • Sonic profile: the noise it makes, from rattles, joints and the action itself
  • Colour: last, even though it is usually the first thing people worry about

Get the first six sorted and colour matters a lot less.

Size and depth

Matching the hatch is the default, but not always the answer. A big fish that is full, or a fish that has shut down, will sometimes take a small lure as an after-dinner mint. Other times a large lure pulled straight through its zone will draw an aggressive reaction bite. It is worth experimenting.

Active barra generally look up to feed, which is why their heads are shaped the way they are. So in most situations you want the lure level with the fish or just above them. The exception is when fish are shut down and sitting on the bottom. Then it pays to drag the lure slowly along the bottom or just above it, even under the fish, and shake it in front of their faces. Barra do not have hands. If something annoys them enough, the only way to deal with it is with their mouths.

Presence, action and noise

Presence is hard to pin down, but you can see it when you compare two lures that dive to the same depth. One has a tight, small wiggle and the other swims looser through the nose. They look different to a fish even before you think about rattles. Timber lures sit differently in the water from moulded plastic ones, and both are very different from soft plastics.

Shape matters too. We like lures that are high and tight: skinny through the body but tall, so they show a big broadside. Barra love eating a big-looking bait, and a broad side is a real bite trigger. A lure that was round as well as tall could not swim with the same action. The skinny build is what lets it twitch so hard.

Sonic profile is presence and action plus the noise of rattle chambers and joints. When barra are shut down and not biting aggressively, they respond better to subtle lures, like a timber hard body or a flick-tail soft plastic. Fish that are up and actively feeding barely notice small changes, and sometimes standing out a little gets you noticed by a hungry one.

A lure with too much rattle can definitely shut fish down or spook them, just like a sounder or a banging esky. Even so, we would never throw the loud ones away. When fish are sulking and you have tried everything else, crash something noisy through them and see if you can wake them up.

The ladder from loud to subtle

Think of your box as a spectrum. At one end is the noisiest barra lure you can buy, a big rattling hard body. At the other end is a fly, which is just a hook with some hair tied to it. The more of that spectrum you cover, the better off you are.

On a day when we think the fish are hungry, we work down the ladder like this:

  • Start with the loud, rattling hard bodies
  • No bites? Move to timber hard bodies
  • Then plastic hard bodies without rattles
  • Then soft plastics, and further down from there

In pressured water, start in the middle or the subtle end. We start with timbers or a soft plastic on a jig head almost every day in Darwin Harbour. If you are getting bites and think you can get more by being aggressive, go louder. If you are getting the odd fish but think you should be getting more, go quieter.

Colour

If we had to pick three colours to fish a whole year, they would be plain white, black and chartreuse. One of each in every lure you carry and you would do pretty well.

  • Dirty or muddy water: stand out. Bright or fluoro colours get attention, and threadfin in particular love a fluoro lure in muddy water. Very dark colours like black or purple throw a strong silhouette
  • Clear, tannin or clean coastal water: go natural. The fish are generally more wary

Trolling depth comes from line length

A lure only reaches its rated depth with enough line out. With 10 to 15 metres out, a lure rated to 3 metres might only get down about a metre. That is fine if the fish are feeding at a metre, but it is not where you think you are fishing.

As a general rule, 30 to 40 metres back is a good trolling distance. It also keeps the lure away from the motor noise. Under 3 metres the sweet spot is pretty forgiving. Deeper than that, too much line creates drag and stops the lure getting down, which is why deep trollers often run their lures shorter. Once you have the right distance, mark the line with a black marker so you can let it out to the same point every time.

A starter box

Timber lures are worth having. They cover a step on the ladder that plastics do not, and they are deadly in billabongs and freshwater for twitching around snag piles. A few to start with:

  • A timber barra minnow that dives to about 3 metres
  • A slim Rapala-style minnow working the 1.5 to 2 metre range
  • A timber spearhead-style mullet imitation diving to about 2 metres, a long-time favourite at Corroboree Billabong

For soft plastics, scented ones like Gulps have outfished unscented plastics for us most of the time. Added scent on a plastic has given us more bites on some days and made no difference on others. At the very least it masks other smells, so it cannot hurt.

For jig heads, a quarter-ounce 3/0 extra-heavy with a keeper collar is a great all-rounder. The collar holds stretchy plastics straight. Drop to lighter heads on your light spin outfit. For plastics bigger than that, we rig on a large worm hook and add a ball sinker on the loop knot to adjust the depth. Lightly weighted and rigged weedless like that, big paddle tails slow-trolled on the electric motor are a quick way to search a long weed bed.

On finesse lures, upgrading the trebles often ruins the action. Swap them for singles of a similar weight instead. As an old bloke once said, you only need two lures: a Gold Bomber and a spare. He was mostly joking, but the point stands. You only catch fish on what you tie on, so tie on something you trust and fish it well.

Common questions

What is the most important thing when choosing a barra lure?

Presentation, meaning whether you are casting or trolling, because some lures only suit one. After that comes size, depth, presence, action and sound. Colour comes last. Get the first six right and colour matters much less.

What colour lure is best for barramundi?

If you had only three, pick plain white, black and chartreuse. In dirty water, go bright or fluoro, or very dark for a strong silhouette. In clear or tannin water, natural colours usually work best on wary fish.

How far behind the boat should I troll a barra lure?

About 30 to 40 metres is a good general distance. With only 10 to 15 metres out, a lure rated to 3 metres may only reach about a metre. Mark your line once you find the right distance so you can repeat it.

Do rattling lures scare barramundi?

They can. Too much rattle can spook fish or shut them down, especially in pressured water. Start subtle there. Keep the loud lures for hungry fish, or as a last resort to wake up fish that will not bite anything else.
