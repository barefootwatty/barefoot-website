Choke points: where barra wait for the bait

If you only learn to read one kind of structure up here, make it this one. Choke points are the most consistent barra ground in the Top End, and they are the easiest to find before you even leave the house.

A choke point is any spot where a lot of water has to squeeze through a small gap. A bottleneck between rocks, a saddle running off a point, a drain coming off a flat, a gap under a leaning tree. The tide does the work: it pushes bait into the gap, the gap concentrates them, and for a few minutes they have nowhere to hide.

Barra know this better than we do. They will sit below a choke point and wait, and when they are not actively feeding there they are rarely far away.

Why they work

Bait moving with the tide follows cover — a bank, a weed line, a mangrove edge. A choke point takes that cover away. To keep going, the bait has to lift up over a saddle, or funnel through a narrow gap, and expose itself in open water. That is the whole mechanism. Everything else is detail.

Shallow points with deeper water either side are the clearest version of it. They are obvious, they hold fish year after year, and they are worth checking every trip.

Finding them before you leave

Most of this work happens on a screen. Google Earth, your charts and your sounder will show you saddles, points and narrow entrances well before you burn any fuel. Look for:

  • A big area of water draining into a small one — the bigger the mismatch, the better
  • Shallow ground with deeper water on both sides
  • Creek and river mouths that narrow, then open into a hole or an eddy
  • Rock bars that only show themselves on a big low

Some of the best ones will not show up on a chart at all. A gap a metre wide does not photograph. That is why, when you idle past a rocky point, it is worth trimming up and going in for a look rather than giving it a wide berth. It might take a few trips to work out when you can get in there and when you cannot, but that knowledge lasts.

When to be there

Timing matters more than technique here. There is not a lot of specialised casting involved — it is mostly about standing in the right place at the right hour.

Generally the last half of the tide, and particularly the last two hours of the run-out, is when it all rushes through and the bite switches on. On the turn, the bait that survived pushes back up on the first of the incoming, and you can get a second short window.

Around the mouths of the bigger rivers, the run-out funnels bait through the narrows and into the eddy that forms behind. Off a rocky point on a big low, the same thing happens on a smaller scale — and it is not only barra waiting. Cod, golden snapper, blue salmon, queenfish and big tarpon all use the same gaps.

The freshwater version

The same reading works in impoundments, and it is a trick most Territorians never bother with because we do not have many. Find where an arm narrows, or where the land juts out and forces bait up and over, and you have the same set-up.

First and last light are the times. Bait moves between deep and shallow water, the bigger fish patrol the pinch, and you can sit off it quietly and work vibes down the face or run a surface lure across it as the light goes.

Quietly is the operative word. The blokes chasing big Murray cod down south will fish in socks so they are not banging around the boat, or anchor up hours early, or walk in and fish from the bank. That is not fussiness. A fish that has moved shallow specifically to feed is on high alert, and it does not take much to put it off.

The high-tide version

Where you have high banks with grassland behind them, a big spring tide can push right over the top and then drain back off — a small, artificial run-off. The theory is sound and the timing is everything: it works best after a stretch of dry weather, when whatever is living in the back depressions is ready to leave, and it works better again with a bit of rain about.

Same with the barrages. Once the tides get big enough to push over the top, the fish from the fresh side come down to meet the dirty water pushing in, and the salty fish come up to meet them. Sit off it and wait for the push.

Be honest about scale, though. A funnel is only worth fishing if enough bait is being forced through it to be worth a barra’s while. A tiny drain off a footy-oval-sized flat is a choke point in theory, but there may be nothing there to concentrate. Pick the one where the biggest area drains into the smallest gap.

What to take from it

Choke points are the most transferable thing in Top End barra fishing. The specific spots do not matter — every system has its own, and the good ones change as banks collapse and rivers flush out. What matters is the shape: big water into small water, cover running out, bait exposed, fish waiting below.

Once you can see that shape on a chart, you can walk into a system you have never fished and have a decent idea of where to start.

Common questions

What is a choke point in barra fishing?

Any spot where a lot of water has to squeeze through a small gap — a bottleneck between rocks, a saddle off a point, a drain coming off a flat, or a gap under a leaning tree. The tide pushes bait into the gap, the gap concentrates them, and barramundi sit below and wait.

When is the best time to fish a choke point?

The last half of the tide, and particularly the last two hours of the run-out, when the water rushing through switches the bite on. You often get a second shorter window on the first of the incoming, as bait that survived pushes back up.

How do you find choke points before you get on the water?

On a screen. Google Earth, your charts and your sounder will show saddles, points and narrow entrances. Look for a big area of water draining into a small one, shallow ground with deeper water either side, creek mouths that narrow into a hole, and rock bars that only show on a big low.

Do choke points work in freshwater impoundments?

Yes, and most Top End anglers never try it. Find where an arm narrows or where land juts out and forces bait up and over. First and last light are the times, fished quietly — work vibes down the face or run a surface lure across it as the light goes.
